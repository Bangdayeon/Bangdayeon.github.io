## Plot
The protagonist Ronit ran away from her traditional Jewish family some time ago. Hearing that her father has died, she comes back briefly, and the film starts as she meets her childhood friends Dovid and Esti. While Ronit was away Dovid and Esti got married, and the reason Ronit's face falls on seeing them is that she and Esti used to be in love. Traditional Judaism has a lot of laws that can feel suffocating — nappy-like underclothes, wearing a wig, mandatory sex every Friday, the expectation of seven children. Ronit hated that and fled; Esti married thinking that if she had to marry someone here, her friend Dovid was the better option.

Then these two fearlessly kiss on the street in a traditional Jewish neighbourhood;; get caught by a neighbour, and Dovid learns they love each other. But Dovid had proposed knowing that Ronit and Esti had been in love. So I wasn't sure what to make of him — until the pregnant Esti says she wants to raise the child freely and asks Dovid for her freedom. Dovid leaves without a word, and then at the funeral where he is to receive her father's position as rabbi, he speaks about freedom of choice, referring to her father's earlier sermon, looks at Esti and says "You're free." He also declines the position, saying he isn't ready. And the next morning Esti wakes on the living-room sofa in comfortable pyjamas instead of the nappy-like underclothes, with her own tousled hair instead of a wig — against the law, unlike any other day. It's so ordinary, and yet for Dovid and Esti, born and raised and devout in a traditional Jewish community where all of that was forbidden, I wonder how enormous a world it must have broken open. Esti doesn't leave with Ronit either. All three have now freely made their own choices, and will live the lives that follow from them.
