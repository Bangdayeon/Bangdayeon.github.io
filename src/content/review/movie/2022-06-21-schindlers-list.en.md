## Plot

Schindler is a businessman who loves money.

Because Jewish workers could be hired cheaply at the time, his company employed only Jewish workers — until at some point the Jewish population is herded into one district and a set number are taken to the camps every day.

At first they weren't taken if they had a work certificate, so Schindler diligently secured certificates for his people, but later even those became useless and they simply started massacring and taking people away.

Schindler doesn't know this until, out riding with his wife on a hill, he sees the massacre happening in the Jewish quarter. The child in red on the poster is the one who catches his eye then — a small girl who has lost her parents and is wandering alone looking for somewhere to hide. The film runs in black and white throughout, and only she has colour.

Anyway, seeing that, Schindler feels something and turns his horse. After that he keeps hiring people from the camps as workers and even bribes his way into building a camp to house Jewish people. That camp, of course, has no soldiers' violence and no summary executions, unlike the others, under the banner that the workers are Schindler's essential assets.

It was probably possible to bring in that many Jewish people because Schindler was a wealthy businessman and a socialiser who was close to the upper ranks, and because his company made goods supplied to the army.

## Afterwards

The biggest thing that changed Schindler's heart was the child in red. The girl who caught his eye during the massacre in the Jewish quarter is later carried as a corpse to the place where Jewish bodies are burned, and falls into the fire — and Schindler sees it. After that, he puts saving Jewish lives above money, pulling as many people as he can out of the camps and onto trains bound for his factory in his hometown. There's a tense stretch on the way: men and women were split across separate trains, and the women's train is misrouted to Auschwitz, where they are almost all shaved, changed and sent into the gas shower. Fortunately Schindler races over at the critical moment and takes them away, saying they are all his workers.

In the end every Jewish person named on Schindler's list arrives safely at his factory, keeps the Sabbath, and passes through to 1945 without the soldiers' threat.

When the war ends the Nazis order the soldiers to kill all the Jewish people in the camps, but after Schindler's speech — that the war is over now and they can go home to their families — none of them die and the soldiers simply leave.

Schindler had a role within the Nazi apparatus, so he had to flee, and just before he does the Jewish workers give him a gold ring made from their gold teeth. Looking at the metal badge on his coat and at his car, he weeps with regret that if he had sold these he could have saved eleven more people, and then leaves. Schindler is never punished, and the film ends with those Jewish people and their descendants visiting his grave and laying stones on it.

The fact that it's based on a true story is remarkable.

Schindler, riding high, used his power in that razor's-edge place and bribed his way into saving Jewish lives.

The main antagonist is Amon Göth of the SS. A few of the things he does:

A Jewish woman in charge of construction comes to plead that the engineering is wrong and it must all be torn down and rebuilt from scratch — so he has her killed immediately, and then does what she said. (Why kill her then;)

He gets up in the morning at his house, aims from the balcony at the camp below, and shoots a slow-working prisoner dead.

And while loving a Jewish woman, he clings to that obsession with race, telling her she seduced him, and in the end nothing changes.

Once, while working at school and skimming through newspapers, a paragraph about totalitarianism stuck with me.

## The peculiar language of totalitarianism

In the Germany of that period, assassination was called a "special measure", torture "intensified interrogation", and being sent to a concentration camp "evacuation". "Fanatical" carried a positive sense when it described themselves and a negative one when it described the enemy. Coining and spreading a range of stock phrases to attack the enemy — "alien stock", "the eternal Jew" — also belongs to the linguistic habits peculiar to totalitarians.

Because Korea went through Japanese occupation, I learned a great deal about how much losing your language damages a people's spirit toward their country, and about the efforts made to protect the language — but I don't think I'd ever considered the reverse, of slightly altering an existing language in order to rule with it.

The reason Amon Göth could love a Jewish woman was probably that he didn't genuinely believe Jewish people deserved to be treated as people who ought to die. But he had dissolved into Nazi totalitarianism deeply enough to suppress that, and was certain enough of his own actions, that he could kill that many Jewish people without a flicker and hate even the one he loved.

That so many people were collectively that far gone doesn't feel real.
